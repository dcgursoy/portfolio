Autonomous tunnel boring machine controls

I joined Penn Hyperloop in September 2025 as a power and controls engineer on its tunnel boring machine. On the 2026 machine I owned the PLC logic and designed the 480 V power distribution and safety chain.

The rebuild moved the supply from 240 V to 480 V three-phase. It raised the installed drive rating from 9.7 kW to 29.5 kW. The team placed second at The Boring Company's 2026 Not-a-Boring Competition, with 0.457 m of tunnel. In August 2026 I became controls engineering lead for the 2027 machine, which is in design.

The problem

Penn Hyperloop builds an autonomous tunnel boring machine for The Boring Company's Not-a-Boring Competition. The 2025 machine lost dig time to breaker trips near full power. The 2026 rebuild had to deliver about 30 kW and end every fault with drive power off.

What I built

Our power and controls subteam built the 2026 system together. I owned the PLC logic and led bring-up and integration testing.

A Siemens S7-1214C runs the autonomous drilling sequence. I wrote it in TIA Portal as a PackML dig cycle state machine. A fault or emergency stop forces Aborting from any state.

I configured the three G120 PM250 drives: motor data, ramp times, current limits and telegram setup. I built the PROFINET link that commands them and the Modbus RTU link to sensors and auxiliary devices.

I designed the 480 V power distribution and safety chain. Power enters through an 80 A GFCI breaker, a disconnect, a 96 A line reactor and the main contactor.

A safety relay holds the contactor closed only while a two-channel emergency stop and a PLC permissive are healthy. The hardwired stop chain removes drive power independently of the PLC program.

Results

We placed second at the 2026 Not-a-Boring Competition in Bastrop, Texas. The Boring Company lists 0.457 m of tunnel for us and 3.277 m for the winner. We had zero safety incidents across all of our field testing.

The 2025 breaker trips stopped after we installed the line reactor on the drive input to cut inrush and harmonic current. Our enclosure heat balance called for about 65 CFM, and we fitted a 155 CFM fan.

On campus we ran only small-scale, unloaded tests. Full-system testing waited for the competition site, where we hit GFCI trips, Modbus communication errors and drive overheating. For the GFCI trips we dried and resealed the machine-side connectors.

The Modbus errors turned out to be noise from drive switching. The RS-485 line shared a cable path with the motor leads and had no proper termination.

We rerouted the line in shielded twisted pair and added the missing 120 Ω termination with bias resistors. I slowed the bus down and changed the PLC's stop rule from one missed poll to three. After these changes, Modbus timeouts stopped aborting the dig.

Despite that heat balance, the G120 drives overheated under load in direct sun at Bastrop. The PM250 heatsinks hit their temperature limit, and the drives threw overtemperature alarms and then faults.

The enclosure was not getting enough air, so we improved its airflow. When a drive ran hot we slowed the advance to cut motor current. After that the drives stopped tripping on temperature.

Limits and next steps

The tunnel was 0.457 m against a 30 m design length, and the 2025 machine dug 1.5 m.

The 2027 rules add steering. As controls lead I own the steering electronics, the split between the PLC and an ESP32 layer, and the safety fault responses. Steering uses four Progressive Automations PA-HD3A linear actuators with Hall-effect position feedback.

Leading the controls subteam

I lead a controls subteam of five and onboard new members each year. I wrote and taught a 90-minute PLC fundamentals workshop.
